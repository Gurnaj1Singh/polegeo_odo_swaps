  First, why Faster-LIO is only ~1× real-time here

  This is the key diagnosis before picking a replacement: it's your point 
  density, not the algorithm being "slow." The OS-2-128 at 1024×10 emits
  ~131,000 points/scan. Published Faster-LIO benchmarks that hit ~350 FPS are on
  a Livox Mid-360 (~15,000 pts/scan) — ~9× sparser. Every ESIKF-family LIO
  (FAST-LIO2, Faster-LIO, Point-LIO, iG-LIO…) is dominated by nearest-neighbour
  cost that scales with point count, so on dense 128-beam clouds they all
  collapse toward real-time on a laptop CPU. Two consequences:

  - A different CPU algorithm buys you a constant factor (≈2–4×), not an order 
  of magnitude, unless you also cut point count.
  - The cheapest speedup requires no new algorithm at all: raise
  point_filter_num / filter_size_surf (more aggressive voxel downsampling). Your
  notes say 4→2 barely changed accuracy — so going the other way (e.g.
  point_filter_num 4→6–8, or a coarser surf voxel) is likely free speed for a
  car on open Nordic roads. Worth trying before you re-integrate anything.

  Tier 1 — CPU drop-ins (same ESIKF family as Faster-LIO, lowest integration 
  cost)

  Because you already have the Docker + config + trajectory→CSV bridge working
  for Faster-LIO, these three swap in with almost the same interface (same input
  topics, same yaml style, same output pose stream):
  - Super-LIO — the strongest pure-speed CPU pick: 3.7–4.2× faster than
  FAST-LIO2, ~73% faster than Faster-LIO, and best average accuracy (0.74 m
  RMSE) in its 2025 paper. Uses an "OctVox" 8-point-per-voxel map — which also
  directly attacks your dense-cloud problem.
  - iG-LIO — explicitly benchmarked as more efficient than Faster-LIO with
  comparable accuracy; GICP formulation tends to be a touch more accurate on
  planar/structured geometry.
  - Point-LIO — same lab as FAST-LIO/Faster-LIO, so most similar to what you
  have. Removes in-frame distortion entirely and is exceptionally robust; speed
  is comparable rather than dramatically better, but it's the safest "more
  trustworthy" upgrade.

  Tier 2 — GPU-accelerated (this is my recommendation for your box)

  - GLIM (koide3, MIT, ROS1+ROS2) — tightly-coupled range-inertial, with
  CUDA-accelerated scan-matching. This is the one option that breaks the
  "constant factor" ceiling on dense clouds, because the OS-2-128's 131k-point
  matching is exactly what the GPU eats. Your RTX 3050 Ti (4 GB) is modest but
  plenty for GLIM's scan-matching factors. It also adds loop closure + global
  bundle-style optimization, which is the only thing on this list that would
  actually attack your other problem — the ~450 m yaw drift over 10 km.
  Downside: heavier to set up than a FAST-LIO clone (CUDA toolkit, gtsam), and
  you'd move off Docker-noetic to a CUDA image.

  What none of these will fix

  Your ~450 m global yaw drift is IMU-limited dead-reckoning drift, not an
  odometry-speed problem. A faster/locally-more-accurate front end (Super-LIO,
  iG-LIO) shaves it modestly, but the real cure is loop closure or GNSS fusion —
  which only GLIM provides out of the box here. Your existing pole-correction +
  GNSS anchoring already handles this downstream, so don't expect a new
  odometry to move the global number much.

  My recommendation

  1. Free win first (today): bump Faster-LIO's downsampling (point_filter_num ↑,
  coarser filter_size_surf) and re-check accuracy. On a car this likely gets
  you to 2–3× real-time with negligible accuracy loss — no re-integration.
  2. Best speed/accuracy CPU swap: Super-LIO — fastest of the CPU family and
  highest accuracy, and it slots into your existing Docker/CSV workflow.
  3. If you want to use the GPU and also tackle drift: GLIM — the only path to a
  real order-of-magnitude speedup on dense OS-2-128, plus built-in loop
  closure.

  the same way you did Faster-LIO vs FastReg. Want me to write this up as a doc

  trajectory→CSV bridge to drop into your existing runner?
